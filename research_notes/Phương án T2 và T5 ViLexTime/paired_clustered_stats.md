# Statistics for Paired + Clustered Evaluation Data (NLP/IR Benchmarks)

Scope note: all symbols are defined where first used. Years are given for every
citation; core statistical methodology is often pre-2018 and that is noted
explicitly. Where the literature disagrees or offers no convention, that is

## Q1. Design effect, effective sample size, ICC for binary outcomes, unequal clusters

### Takeaway

The standard design effect is `DE = 1 + (m − 1)ρ` with effective sample size
`n_eff = n / DE`; it assumes equal cluster size `m` and an exchangeable
(equicorrelated) within-cluster correlation `ρ ≥ 0` for a simple mean/proportion
under one level of clustering. With unequal cluster sizes `m` is replaced either
by the harmonic mean of cluster sizes or, equivalently in the Moulton form, by
`m̄(CV² + 1)` where `CV` is the coefficient of variation of cluster sizes. For a
binary outcome the ICC is most commonly estimated by the one-way ANOVA
(method-of-moments) estimator, which Ridout, Demétrio & Firth (1999) show is
legitimate for binary data.

### Cited Findings

- **Design effect (equal clusters).** "For equal cluster sizes: **DE = 1 + (m − 1)ρ**", where `m` = number of subjects per cluster and `ρ` = intracluster correlation coefficient. This is Equation 2 of the paper. — [Killip S, Mahfoud Z, Pearce K. "What Is an Intracluster Correlation Coefficient? Crucial Concepts for Primary Care Researchers." *Ann Fam Med* 2004;2(3):204–208](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1466680/)
- **Effective sample size.** Equation 3 of the same paper: **ESS = mk / DE**, where `mk` is the total sample size (`k` clusters of `m` subjects) and `DE` is the design effect. — [Killip et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1466680/)
- **ICC definition.** ρ is "the between-cluster variability divided by the sum of the within-cluster and between-cluster variabilities", i.e. `ρ = σ²_b / (σ²_b + σ²_w)`. — [Killip et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1466680/)
- **Typical magnitude, and the need to estimate it.** "Usually, values of ρ are between 0.01 and 0.02 in human studies"; "The calculation of ρ usually requires a pilot study." — [Killip et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1466680/)
- **ANOVA (method-of-moments) ICC estimator, valid for binary data.** `ρ̂ = (BMS − WMS) / (BMS + (m₀ − 1)·WMS)`, where `BMS` = between-cluster mean square, `WMS` = within-cluster mean square, and `m₀` = adjusted mean cluster size for unequal cluster sizes. "This type 3 ANOVA method can be used with binary data (Ridout et al. 1999)." Full citation: Ridout MS, Demétrio CGB, Firth D (1999), "Estimating intraclass correlation for binary data", *Biometrics* 55:137–148. — [clus.rho, `fishmethods` R package documentation](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/fishmethods/html/clus.rho.html)
- **Three families of ICC estimators for clustered binary data.** "There are three classes of methods to estimate the ICC for clustered binary data: method of moment estimation, ANOVA, and resampling"; Ridout, Demétrio & Firth (1999) compare their relative weaknesses and advantages. — [search synthesis over R/`fishmethods` docs and Ridout et al. 1999](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/fishmethods/html/clus.rho.html)
- **Regression-side equivalent (the Moulton factor), with the exact unequal-cluster correction.** For the `k`-th regressor, the default OLS variance estimate `s²(X'X)⁻¹` "should be inflated by `τ_k ≃ 1 + ρ_{x_k} ρ_u (N̄_g − 1)`", where `ρ_{x_k}` is the within-cluster correlation of the regressor, `ρ_u` the within-cluster error correlation, `N̄_g` the average cluster size. "The result is exact if clusters are of equal size ('balanced' clusters) and `ρ_{x_k} = 1` for all regressors (Kloek, 1981)." And: "For clusters of unequal size replace `(N̄_g − 1)` ... by `((V[N_g]/N̄_g) + N̄_g − 1)`; see Moulton (1986, p.387)." The inflation factor increases in (1) within-cluster correlation of the regressor, (2) within-cluster correlation of the error, (3) number of observations per cluster; "there is no cluster problem if any one of the following occur: `ρ_{x_k} = 0` or `ρ_u = 0` or `N̄_g = 1`." — [Cameron AC, Miller DL. "A Practitioner's Guide to Cluster-Robust Inference." *Journal of Human Resources* 2015;50(2):317–372, §II, eq. (6)](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **How big τ can get even with small ρ.** Moulton's (1990) worked case: log-earnings regression, March CPS, `N = 18,946`, state-level regressor, `G = 49` states, `ρ̂_u = 0.032`, average group size `387` ⇒ `τ_k ≃ 1 + 1 × 0.032 × 386 = 13.3` (a 13-fold variance inflation, i.e. standard errors understated by ~3.6×). — [Cameron & Miller 2015](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Unequal-cluster design effect, two standard forms** (secondary source, see caveat): harmonic mean substitution `m̃ = k / Σ(1/m_i)`, or the CV form `DE = 1 + ((CV² + 1)·m̄ − 1)·ρ` attributed to Eldridge et al. (2006), with the note that "the harmonic mean is always less than or equal to the arithmetic mean, which means using the arithmetic mean would underestimate the design effect and lead to an underpowered study." Worked example given: 1,500 respondents in 60 clusters of 25 with `ICC = 0.05` ⇒ `DEFF ≈ 1 + 24 × 0.05 = 2.2`, `n_eff ≈ 1,500 / 2.2 ≈ 682`. — [MetricGate, "Design Effect (DEFF) in Surveys" / "Sample Size for Cluster-Randomized Trials"](https://metricgate.com/blogs/design-effect-deff-clustered-surveys/); **caveat: this is a commercial statistics website, not a peer-reviewed source.** The underlying reference it names is Eldridge SM, Ashby D, Kerry S (2006), "Sample size for cluster randomized trials: effect of coefficient of variation of cluster size and analysis method", *Int J Epidemiol* 35:1292–1300, which I did not read directly.

### Inferences

- **The two "unequal cluster size" corrections are algebraically the same object.** Moulton's (1986) peer-reviewed correction `V[N_g]/N̄_g + N̄_g − 1` equals `N̄_g·CV² + N̄_g − 1 = N̄_g(CV² + 1) − 1`, since `CV² = V[N_g]/N̄_g²`. That is exactly the Eldridge-style `DE = 1 + (m̄(CV² + 1) − 1)ρ`. So the CV form can be used with a primary citation (Moulton 1986, via Cameron & Miller 2015) even though I could only verify the Eldridge attribution on a secondary site. (Derivation mine, from the quoted Cameron & Miller formula.)
- **Recommended form for a benchmark author.** Report `DE = 1 + (m_adj − 1)·ρ̂` and `n_eff = n / DE`, with `m_adj = m̄(CV² + 1)` where `m̄ = n/k` and `CV = sd(m_i)/m̄`; state `ρ̂`, its estimator (ANOVA/MoM), `k`, and the cluster-size vector. The harmonic-mean substitution is the more conservative-looking of the two only because it enters a different place in the derivation; the CV form is the one that *increases* DE with size imbalance and is therefore the appropriate conservative choice for a benchmark with highly uneven documents.
- **Assumption that most often fails on benchmark data.** The formula presumes one nesting level and exchangeability within cluster. A benchmark where items are nested in documents *and* paired by date has two dependence structures at once (clustering and pairing); the design effect handles only the clustering. See Q4/Q6 for how the pairing enters.

### Gaps

- I did not find a source stating the admissible range of ICC for a binary outcome as a function of prevalence (the attainable `ρ` is bounded by `p`, which matters when prevalence is extreme, e.g. ~85%). The ICC-for-binary literature (Ridout et al. 1999) is the right place to look, but I could not access its full text.
- I could not read Donner & Klar (2000, *Design and Analysis of Cluster Randomization Trials in Health Research*) or Kish (1965, *Survey Sampling*) directly. Kish (1965) is the standard origin citation for "design effect" and Donner & Klar (2000) for the cluster-trial treatment of `n_eff`; both should be cited from their canonical bibliographic entries, not from a secondary paraphrase, and neither was verifiable here.
- I did not find a source for Hox's multilevel "50 clusters" rule of thumb (commonly attributed to Hox 2010, *Multilevel Analysis*); the closest verified statement is Cameron & Miller's `G = 50` consensus (Q2).

## Q2. What is the practical floor on inference when items sit in 4–8 clusters?

### Takeaway

Yes, there is an explicit and citable statement: as the within-cluster
correlation goes to 1 the effective sample size reduces to `k`, the *number of
clusters*. The econometric literature does not give a hard threshold but reports
a working consensus of `G ≈ 50` clusters for reliable cluster-robust inference,
with documented severe size distortion at `G = 5–10`, and a formal "effective
number of clusters" `G*` whose distortion threshold is `G* < 20`. At `k = 4–8`,
no source found supports treating the result as a conventional inferential
estimate.

### Cited Findings

- **The explicit floor statement.** When `ρ = 1` — the theoretical case where "all responses within a cluster are identical" — "the effective sample size therefore reduces to `k`, the number of clusters." — [Killip et al. 2004, *Ann Fam Med*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1466680/)
- **No hard threshold, but "more is better" and a `G = 50` consensus.** "Before proceeding we note that there is no specific point at which we need to worry about few clusters. Instead, 'more is better'. Current consensus appears to be that `G = 50` is enough for state-year panel data." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.A](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Measured over-rejection by number of clusters (two independent simulation studies).** Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004, Table 8): with a policy dummy and high within-cluster correlation, a Wald test using the standard cluster-robust variance estimator (CRVE) and critical value 1.96 had rejection rates of **.063, .058, .080, .115** for `G = 50, 20, 10, 6`. Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008, Table 3), different data-generating process, same test: **.068, .081, .118, .208** for `G = 30, 20, 10, 5`. Nominal size was 0.05 in both. "In both cases the rejection rates would also exceed .05 if the critical value was from the `T(G − 1)` distribution." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.A](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **The two mechanisms behind few-cluster failure.** "First, OLS leads to 'overfitting', with estimated residuals systematically too close to zero compared to the true error terms. This leads to a downwards-biased cluster-robust variance matrix estimate. The second problem is that even with bias-correction, the use of fitted residuals ... leads to over-rejection (and confidence intervals that are too narrow) if the critical values are from the standard normal or even the `T(G − 1)` distribution." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.A](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **"Effective number of clusters" `G*` and its distortion threshold.** Carter, Schnepel & Steigerwald (2013) propose `G* = G / (1 + δ)`, with `δ = G Σ_g {(γ_g − γ̄)² / γ̄²}`, `γ_g = e'_k (X'X)⁻¹ X'_g Ω_g X_g (X'X)⁻¹ e_k`, `γ̄ = (1/G) Σ_g γ_g`. "The quantity `δ` measures cluster heterogeneity, which disappears if `γ_g = γ` for all `g`"; heterogeneity "can arise for many reasons, including variation in `N_g`, variation in `X_g` and variation in `Ω_g` across clusters." Critically: "In simulations using standard normal critical values, Carter et al. (2013) find that test size distortion occurs for `G* < 20`." Note `G*` is coefficient-specific. — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.D.3, eq. (27)](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Minimum adjustments even when you do proceed.** "One should at a minimum use `T(G − 1)` critical values and `V̂_clu[β̂]` ... with residuals scaled by `√c` where `c = G/(G − 1)`." And from Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008): "at the very least one should provide some small sample correction of standard errors, such as magnifying the residuals ... by a factor `√(G/(G − 1))` and using a `T` distribution with `G` or fewer degrees of freedom (we arbitrarily used `G − 2`)." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf); [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller. "Bootstrap-Based Improvements for Inference with Clustered Errors." NBER Technical Working Paper 344, 2007 / *Review of Economics and Statistics* 2008;90(3):414–427, Conclusion](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- **How much `T(G − 1)` matters.** "For example with `G = 10` for a two-sided test at level 0.05 the critical value for `T(9)` is 2.262 rather than 1.960, and if `w = 1.960` the p-value based on `T(9)` is 0.082 rather than 0.05." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.D.1](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **When all regressors are cluster-invariant, inference is literally at the cluster level.** "For models that include `L` regressors that are invariant within cluster, Donald and Lang (2007) provide a rationale for using the `T(G − L)` distribution. If clusters are balanced and all regressors are invariant within cluster then the OLS estimator in the model `y_ig = x'_g β + u_ig` is equivalent to OLS estimation in the grouped model `ȳ_g = x'_g β + ū_g`." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.D](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Unbalanced clusters reduce the effective cluster count further.** Cameron & Miller cite theory "that also indicates that the effective number of clusters is reduced when `N_g` varies across clusters", with supporting simulations in MacKinnon & Webb (2013). — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.B](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)

### Inferences

- With `k = 8` documents (and `k = 4` for the cleaner subset), the Carter et al. `G* < 20` distortion threshold is violated by construction, regardless of how many items each document contributes. Document-count, not item-count, is the binding constraint.
- A defensible way to state the floor: *"Under the exchangeable design-effect model, `n_eff` is bounded above by `n` and tends to `k` as `ρ → 1` (Killip et al. 2004). With `k = 8` (resp. 4), no amount of within-document item generation raises `n_eff` above 8 (resp. 4) if items within a document are strongly correlated."* That is a direct consequence of the quoted ρ = 1 statement, not an extrapolation.
- The Donald & Lang (2007) result gives the cleanest principled framing for a benchmark: if the thing you are comparing varies at the document level (e.g. document-level normative structure drives correctness), the honest unit of analysis *is* the document, and the degrees of freedom are `k − L`.

### Gaps

- No source found gives an accepted minimum `k` below which reporting an interval is considered improper. The literature gives distortion magnitudes (above), not a prohibition. Any statement like "4 clusters is too few to do inference" is a defensible reading of those magnitudes but is not itself a quotable rule.
- Carter, Schnepel & Steigerwald (2013) was unpublished at the time of Cameron & Miller (2015); I did not verify whether a journal version exists with a different threshold.

## Q3. Cluster bootstrap: procedure, failure modes, minimum cluster count, alternatives

### Takeaway

The pairs cluster bootstrap resamples **whole clusters with replacement, keeping
every observation inside a sampled cluster** — clusters only, not
clusters-then-items. It is documented to fail badly with few clusters (rejection
rate 0.005 against a nominal 0.05 at `G = 6`), and the recommended replacement
is the **wild cluster bootstrap-t with the null imposed and Rademacher weights**,
switching to Webb's (2013) six-point weights below `G = 10`. Below about `G = 11`
the bootstrap p-value is *discrete*: at most `2^(G−1)` distinct values, so the
p-value itself has coarse resolution (`G = 5` ⇒ resolution 1/16 = 0.0625).

### Cited Findings

- **The pairs cluster bootstrap procedure (clusters only).** "The simplest cluster resampling method is the pairs cluster resampling ... we form `G` clusters `{(y*₁, X*₁), ..., (y*_G, X*_G)}` by resampling with replacement `G` times from the original sample of clusters. This method has the advantage of being applicable to a wide range of estimators, not just OLS." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.1](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Pairs cluster bootstrap does not fix few-cluster over-rejection; it can fail catastrophically.** "Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller (2008) found that in Monte Carlos with few clusters the pairs cluster bootstrap did not eliminate test over-rejection." In the Bertrand–Duflo–Mullainathan replication, "The pairs cluster percentile-t bootstrap fails with few clusters, with rejection rate of only 0.005 when `G = 6`" against nominal 0.05 — i.e. it becomes radically *under*-powered, not merely mis-sized. — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.1 and §X](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Named failure modes of the pairs cluster bootstrap** (all verbatim-sourced): (1) **outlier cluster** — "the resulting histogram may have a big 'mass' that sits separately from the rest of the bootstrap distribution ... one for cases where that cluster is sampled and one for cases where it is not. If this is the case then you know that your results are sensitive to the inclusion of that cluster"; (2) **dummy regressors / no treatment variation in a resample** — "in some samples you can get no or very limited variation in treatment. This can lead to zero or near-zero standard errors ... if the bootstrapped standard errors are zero plus machine precision noise ... very large t-values may result. Then your bootstrap distribution of t-statistics will have really fat tails, and you will not reject anything, even false null hypotheses"; (3) **near-collinear draws** — "you can get huge `β̂*`'s. This can make a bootstrapped standard error seem very large." In cases (2) and (3) the explicit recommendation is: "In this case we suggest using the wild cluster bootstrap." Also, from the 2008 paper: "when there are few clusters some bootstrap resamples may have all clusters with [the same value of the indicator] ... The residual or wild cluster bootstraps do not [have this problem]." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.3 "Bootstrap with Caution"](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf); [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2007/2008, §3.3](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- **Diagnostics you must run when bootstrapping with few clusters.** "an important step when bootstrapping with few clusters is to examine the distribution of bootstrapped values ... (1) basic summary statistics like mean and variance; (2) the sample size to confirm that it is the same as the number of bootstrap replications (no missing values); (3) the largest and smallest handful of values of the distribution; and (4) a histogram of the bootstrapped values." And: "in general if you see missing values in your bootstrapped t-statistics, then you need to figure out why. Don't take your bootstrap results at face value until you know what's going on." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.3](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Wild cluster bootstrap-t, step by step (verbatim).** "First, estimate the main model, imposing (forcing) the null hypothesis `H₀` that you wish to test, to give estimate `β̂_H0` ... Form the residual `ũ_ig = y_i − x'_ig β̂_H0`. Then obtain the `b`-th resample as follows: **1a.** Randomly assign cluster `g` the weight `d_g = −1` with probability 0.5 and the weight `d_g = 1` with probability 0.5. All observations in cluster `g` get the same value of the weight. **1b.** Generate new pseudo-residuals `u*_ig = d_g × ũ_ig`, and hence new outcome variables `y*_ig = x'_ig β̂_H0 + u*_ig`." Then regress `y*_ig` on `x_ig`, compute the Wald statistic `w*_b`, and "The p-value for the test based on the original sample Wald statistic `w` equals the proportion of times that `|w| > |w*_b|`, `b = 1, ..., B`." The weights `d_g = {−1, 1}` "are called Rademacher weights", and Davidson & Flachaire (2008) show they are best in the heteroskedastic case. — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.2](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Confidence intervals from the wild cluster bootstrap require test inversion.** "For the wild bootstrap, the values `w*₁, ..., w*_B` cannot be used directly to form critical values for a confidence interval. Instead they can be used to provide a p-value for testing a hypothesis. To form a confidence interval, one needs to invert a sequence of tests, profiling over a range of candidate null hypotheses `H₀: β = β₀`. For each of these null hypotheses, obtain the p-value. The 95% confidence interval is the set of values of `β₀` for which `p ≥ 0.05`." Practical note: "you may want to ensure that you have the same set of bootstrap draws across candidate hypotheses, so as to not introduce additional bootstrapping noise." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.2](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **p-value discreteness with few clusters — the hardest constraint.** "Because this is done across `G` clusters, there are at most `2^G` possible combinations of the data, so there are at most `2^G` unique values of `w*₁, ..., w*_B`. If there are very few clusters then there is no need to actually bootstrap as we can simply enumerate, with separate estimation for each of the `2^G` possible datasets. Webb (2013) ... shows that there are actually only `2^(G−1)` possible t-statistics in absolute value. Thus with `G = 5` there are at most `2⁴ = 16` possible values ... So if the main test statistic is more extreme than any of these 16 values, for example, then all we know is that the p-value is smaller than `1/16 = 0.0625`. Full enumeration makes this discreteness clear. Bootstrapping without consideration of this issue can lead to inadvertently picking just one point from the interval of equally plausible p-values. As `G` gets to be as large as 11 this concern is less of an issue since `2¹⁰ = 1024`." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.2](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Webb's six-point weights for `G < 10`.** "Webb (2013) proposes greatly reducing the discreteness of p-values with very low `G` by instead using a six-point distribution for the weights `d_g` ... the weights `d_g` have a 1/6 chance of taking each value in `{−√1.5, −√1, −√0.5, √0.5, √1, √1.5}`. In his simulations this method outperforms the two-point wild bootstrap for `G < 10` and is the preferred method to use if `G < 10`." Repeated as a rule: "Fourth, with less than ten clusters the wild cluster bootstrap should use the six-point version of Webb (2013)." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.2–C.3](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **How few clusters the wild/residual cluster bootstrap can handle.** "The main results of this section are that the residual and wild cluster bootstrap methods perform well in cases with as few as 6 clusters. These results stand in contrast to the more pessimistic conclusion about cluster bootstrapping in BDM (2004)." The paper's abstract frames the whole problem as "few (5-30) clusters" and reports "Rejection rates of ten percent using standard methods can be reduced to the nominal size of five percent using our methods." Also: "Fourth, we consider examples with as few as five clusters." — [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2007/2008, Abstract, §1, §5](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- **It is the bootstrap-*t* (percentile-t), not the bootstrap-se, that helps.** "The usual way that the bootstrap is used, to obtain an estimate of the standard error, does not lead to improved inference with few clusters as it does not provide an asymptotic refinement. We focus on the bootstrap-t procedure ... which provides asymptotic refinement. We find that the bootstrap-t procedure can lead to considerable improvement, provided the same method is used in calculating the Wald statistic in the original sample and in the bootstrap resamples." — [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2007/2008, Conclusion](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- **Residual cluster bootstrap and why wild is preferred.** The residual cluster bootstrap "resamples with replacement from the original sample residual vectors" and "has two weaknesses. First, it assumes that the regression error vectors `u_g` are iid, whereas ... we were specifically concerned that the variance matrix `Σ_g` will differ across clusters. Second, it presumes a balanced data set where all clusters are the same size. The wild bootstrap relaxes both these restrictions." — [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2007/2008, §3.4](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- **Impose the null.** "Several authors, particularly Davidson and MacKinnon (1999), advocate use of bootstrap resampling methods that impose the null hypothesis. This is possible using both residual and Wild bootstraps." — [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2007/2008, §3.4](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)
- **Unbalanced clusters break even `G = 50` CRVE, but not the wild bootstrap.** "MacKinnon and Webb (2013) address the issue of unbalanced clusters and find that, even with `G = 50`, tests based on the standard CRVE with `T(G − 1)` critical values can over-reject considerably if the clusters are unbalanced. By contrast, the two-point wild bootstrap with Rademacher weights is generally reliable." — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.2](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Alternatives for very few clusters.** (a) **Cluster-level t-statistic**: Ibragimov & Müller (2010) base inference on `t = √G (β̂_g − β₀)/s_β̂` computed from the `G` cluster-specific estimates — Cameron & Miller describe this as usable with "extremely few clusters, assuming `N_g` is large. However, the requirement that cluster estimates [be obtainable] ..." constrains it; they also note "The conservative test of Ibragimov & Müller (2010) can be used" in the nonlinear case. (b) **Aggregate to cluster means** with `T(G − L)` critical values (Donald & Lang 2007). (c) **Randomization/permutation inference**: Cameron & Miller point to "inference under random permutations of assignment to treated and untreated groups" as an approach for the few-clusters case, and reference "Clustering, Spatial Correlations and Randomization Inference" (*JASA*) in that context. — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.E, §VI.D, §V](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)
- **Bootstrap replication count used in the literature.** CGM used `B = 399` "rather than the recommended `B = 999` or higher" purely for Monte Carlo cost; `B = 999` or more is the recommendation. — [Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2007/2008, §4](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0344.pdf)

### Inferences

- **Answer to "clusters only, or clusters-then-items?"** All verified sources describe cluster-level resampling only: whole clusters drawn with replacement, every observation inside a drawn cluster retained. Nothing in the sources I read supports a second within-cluster resampling stage for the purpose of cluster-robust inference — on the contrary, the point of the wild cluster bootstrap is to *preserve* the within-cluster structure exactly (same `X_g`, only a sign flip applied uniformly to the whole cluster).
- **Answer to "is there a commonly cited minimum like 20/30/50?"** I found no single "minimum clusters for cluster bootstrap" number. What exists instead, and is citable: `G = 50` as a working consensus for CRVE (Cameron & Miller 2015); `G* < 20` as a *measured* size-distortion threshold (Carter et al. 2013); `G < 10` as the switch point to Webb six-point weights; `G ≈ 11` as the point where p-value discreteness stops biting (`2^10 = 1024`); and `G = 6` as the lowest count at which the wild/residual cluster bootstrap was shown to perform acceptably (CGM 2008). An author with 8 clusters sits just above the demonstrated floor; with 4 clusters, `2^3 = 8` distinct `|t|` values means the best achievable p-value resolution is 1/8 = 0.125 — i.e. **a 4-document subset cannot in principle produce `p < 0.05` from a wild cluster bootstrap**, which is a decisive, derivable (from the quoted `2^(G−1)` result) argument for treating it as non-inferential.
- With 8 clusters, full enumeration (`2^8 = 256` sign vectors, or `2^7 = 128` distinct `|t|`) is cheap and strictly better than sampling — Cameron & Miller explicitly recommend enumeration at very low `G`. Best achievable two-sided p-value resolution is then 1/128 ≈ 0.0078, so `p < 0.05` is attainable but the grid is coarse near the threshold.

### Gaps

- **No NLP-facing treatment of the cluster bootstrap was found.** The NLP bootstrap tradition (Koehn 2004; Berg-Kirkpatrick et al. 2012, as cited by Dror et al. 2018) resamples *test items* i.i.d., which is exactly the procedure that is invalid under clustering. I found no ACL-venue paper addressing document-level clustering of benchmark items. This is a genuine literature gap, not a search failure on my part — I searched and found nothing. The report should say so rather than imply NLP guidance exists.
- I did not verify Field & Welsh (2007, *JRSS-B*, "Bootstrapping clustered data"), which is the standard statistical reference distinguishing the one-stage cluster bootstrap from a two-stage (clusters-then-items) bootstrap and giving the conditions under which each is consistent. An author wanting to justify "clusters only" formally should read it; I can only report that the econometric sources I read use clusters-only.
- MacKinnon & Webb's later work on the wild cluster bootstrap's own failure mode (few *treated* clusters) is referenced by Cameron & Miller only as a 2013 working paper; I did not verify the published results or the specific thresholds.

## Q4. McNemar's test: assumptions, and the clustered generalisation

### Takeaway

McNemar's test assumes paired binary observations that are **independent across
pairs**; all its information comes from the discordant pairs. That independence
assumption is exactly what clustering violates, so plain McNemar is *not* valid
on clustered matched-pair data. The accepted clustered generalisations are
Eliasziw & Donner (1991), Obuchowski (1998), Durkalski et al. (2003,
method-of-moments variance), and Yang, Sun & Hardin (2010); the R package
`clust.bin.pair` implements all four and defaults to Yang (2010). All of them
rely on a reasonably large number of clusters for correct Type I error.

### Cited Findings

- **Original McNemar.** McNemar, Q. (1947). "Note on the sampling error of the difference between correlated proportions or percentages." *Psychometrika* 12(2):153–157. — [`clust.bin.pair` reference manual, CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/clust.bin.pair/refman/clust.bin.pair.html)
- **McNemar as a special case of the sign test, and where it sits among NLP tests.** Dror et al. classify it under "sampling-free tests": "The next test is a special case of the sign test for binary classification (or a two-tailed sign test). McNemar's test (McNemar, 1947) ..." They also note Cochran (1950) "generalizes the McNemar's test" to more than two systems, and that "The sign test and its variants consider only pairwise ranks: which algorithm performs better on [each item]", so they discard effect magnitude and are therefore less powerful than Wilcoxon signed-rank where a continuous measure exists. — [Dror R, Baumer G, Shlomov S, Reichart R. "The Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in Natural Language Processing." ACL 2018, §3.2](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **The four clustered generalisations, with full citations.** Implemented in `clust.bin.pair` (R), with Yang (2010) as the package default and McNemar "included for comparison":
  - Eliasziw M, Donner A (1991). "Application of the McNemar test to non-independent matched pair data." *Statistics in Medicine* 10(12):1981–1991.
  - Obuchowski NA (1998). "On the comparison of correlated proportions for clustered data." *Statistics in Medicine* 17(13):1495–1507.
  - Durkalski VL, Palesch YY, Lipsitz SR, Rust PF (2003). "Analysis of clustered matched-pair data." *Statistics in Medicine* 22(15):2417–2428.
  - Yang Z, Sun X, Hardin JW (2010). "A note on the tests for clustered matched-pair binary data." *Biometrical Journal* 52(5):638–652.
  — [`clust.bin.pair` reference manual, CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/clust.bin.pair/refman/clust.bin.pair.html)
- **What Durkalski et al. (2003) does.** The paper "proposes a simple adjustment to the McNemar test for the analysis of clustered matched-pair data using method of moments to calculate a consistent variance estimator." The variance adjustment accounts for "unequal cluster sizes, variability of correlation structures within a cluster (within paired responses in a cluster as well as between paired responses in a cluster), and unequal success probabilities among the clusters." Size and power were compared to two existing methods by Monte Carlo and illustrated on clustered matched-pair data from two clinical studies. — [PubMed record for Durkalski et al. 2003 (*Stat Med* 22(15):2417–2428, DOI 10.1002/sim.1438)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12872299/), paraphrase retrieved via search of that record
- **The method-of-moments (Durkalski) test statistic, with symbols.**
  `Z_EV = Σ_k (x₁₀ₖ − x₀₁ₖ − n_k δ₀) / { Σ_k (x₁₀ₖ − x₀₁ₖ − n_k δ₀)² }^(1/2)`
  where `x₁₀ₖ` = number of discordant pairs in cluster `k` in which system A succeeds and system B fails; `x₀₁ₖ` = number in which B succeeds and A fails; `n_k` = number of matched pairs in cluster `k`; `K` = total number of clusters; `δ₀` = pre-specified non-inferiority margin (set `δ₀ = 0` for a plain two-sided test of marginal homogeneity). "For a large number of clusters, the statistic is approximately normal with mean zero and variance one under the null hypothesis." — [Non-inferiority tests for clustered matched-pair binary data, PMC2717020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717020/)
- **The Obuchowski-style inflation-factor form.** `Z_O = (p̂₁•• − p̂•₁• − δ₀) / {v̂ar(p̂₁•• − p̂•₁• − δ₀)}^(1/2)`, "where the variance accounts for clustering through between- and within-cluster components", and an inflation factor `c̄ = 1 + (n̄_c − 1)ρ̂` where `n̄_c` is the **adjusted mean number of discordant pairs per cluster** and `ρ̂` the estimated intraclass correlation. An adjusted score form (Nam) is `Z_AN = Z_N / {1 + (n_c − 1)ρ̂}^(1/2)`. — [PMC2717020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717020/)
- **All these tests still need many clusters.** That paper "emphasizes that 'a large number of clusters is pivotal to obtain accurate Type 1 error rate' in these tests." — [PMC2717020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717020/)
- **A GEE/marginal-homogeneity alternative exists in software.** `htestClust::mcnemartestClust` — "Test of Marginal Homogeneity for Clustered Data" — provides a clustered test of marginal homogeneity for paired binary data with informative cluster size. — [`mcnemartestClust`, `htestClust` R package documentation](https://rdrr.io/cran/htestClust/man/mcnemartestClust.html)

### Inferences

- **Plain McNemar is invalid here, and the reason is precise.** McNemar's `χ² = (b − c)²/(b + c)` with 1 df treats `b + c` (the discordant count) as the variance of `b − c`, which is the multinomial variance *only if pairs are independent*. Under positive within-document correlation the true variance of `b − c` exceeds `b + c`, so the statistic is inflated and the test anti-conservative. Every clustered generalisation above replaces that denominator: Durkalski with `Σ_k (x₁₀ₖ − x₀₁ₖ)²`, Obuchowski/Nam with an explicit `1 + (n̄_c − 1)ρ̂` inflation factor. (Inference from the quoted formulas; the direction of the bias follows from the design-effect logic of Q1.)
- **Durkalski's statistic is a strict generalisation of McNemar.** Set `δ₀ = 0` and let `d_k = x₁₀ₖ − x₀₁ₖ`. Then `Z_EV = Σ_k d_k / √(Σ_k d_k²)`. If every cluster contains exactly one pair (`n_k = 1`), then `d_k ∈ {−1, 0, +1}`, so `Σ_k d_k = b − c` and `Σ_k d_k² = b + c`, giving `Z_EV = (b − c)/√(b + c)` — exactly McNemar's z, whose square is McNemar's `χ²`. **This algebra is mine**, not quoted; I asked a source whether the reduction holds and got an unreliable negative answer, so treat the derivation as the evidence (it is elementary and checkable) rather than citing anyone for it. Practical consequence: `Σ_k d_k²` is the cluster-robust analogue of "number of discordant pairs", and a benchmark author can report `n_eff,disc = (Σ_k d_k)² / Σ_k d_k²`-style quantities to make the loss visible.
- **Recommended test for clustered matched-pairs binary data.** Based on what is verifiable: Durkalski et al. (2003) if you want a transparent, closed-form, assumption-light statistic that collapses to McNemar (the formula above is implementable in a few lines); Yang, Sun & Hardin (2010) if you want the choice that a maintained software package treats as default after a dedicated comparison of all the alternatives. With `K = 8` (and certainly `K = 4`) **none of them is trustworthy**, because all are justified by `K → ∞` normality and the source explicitly flags that a large number of clusters is pivotal for Type I error.
- Because `δ₀` is built into the Durkalski statistic, the same machinery gives a clustered **non-inferiority / equivalence** test, which is often the more honest question for a small subgroup than a two-sided difference test.

### Gaps

- I could not obtain the full text of Durkalski et al. (2003) (paywalled, *Statistics in Medicine*). The statistic above is reproduced from a later paper that restates it; the original's exact notation, its finite-sample recommendations, and any stated minimum number of clusters are unverified.
- I could not obtain Yang, Sun & Hardin (2010), so I cannot report *why* it is the `clust.bin.pair` default or what it recommends. "The documentation does not provide explicit guidance on which test to use for specific scenarios" — [CRAN refman](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/clust.bin.pair/refman/clust.bin.pair.html). Any claim that Yang (2010) is "the recommended" test would be an inference from a package default, not a cited recommendation.
- I found no source giving an exact (permutation-based) clustered McNemar test. By the logic of Q3, a within-cluster-sign-flip permutation test on the cluster-level differences `d_k` would be the natural exact alternative, but I have no citation for it in this setting, and with `K = 4–8` it inherits the same `2^(K−1)` resolution limit.

## Q5. NLP-specific guidance on significance testing, CIs, and multiple comparisons

### Takeaway

Dror et al. (2018) is the standard ACL-venue protocol: a decision tree keyed on
whether the test statistic's distribution is known and on data size, routing to
parametric tests, then sampling-based (bootstrap / approximate randomization),
then sampling-free (McNemar, sign, Wilcoxon). Søgaard et al. (2014) argue NLP
p-values are badly sensitive to sample size and multiplicity and propose a
far stricter threshold (≈0.0025). For multiple comparisons on *dependent* test
sets, Dror et al. (2018) explicitly recommend the replicability-analysis
framework of Dror et al. (2017) with the Bonferroni-based estimator — the
closest thing in the NLP literature to guidance for correlated subgroups.

### Cited Findings

- **The protocol and its decision tree (verbatim structure).** "Does the test statistic come from a known distribution?" → **Yes**: "Use a parametric test". → **No**: "Is the data size small?" → **Yes**: "Use bootstrap or randomization test"; **No**: "Use sampling-free non-parametric test". Rationale given: "If the distribution of the test statistic is known, then parametric tests are most appropriate. These tests are more statistically powerful and less computationally intensive ... When the distribution of the test statistic is unknown, the first non-parametric family of choice is that of sampling-based tests. These tests consider the actual values of the evaluation measures and are not restricted to higher order properties (e.g. ranks) ... their statistical power is hence higher." — [Dror et al. 2018, ACL, §3.3 and Figure 1](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **Bootstrap vs permutation in NLP, and the small-test-set caveat.** "Pitman's permutation test ... estimates the test statistic distribution under the null hypothesis by calculating the values of this statistic under all possible labellings (permutations) of the [data] ... permutation tests are computationally intensive due to the exponentially large number of possible permutations. In practice, approximate randomization tests are used where a pre-defined limited number of permutations are drawn from the space of all possible permutations, without replacements." The paired bootstrap "is very similar to approximate randomization ... with replacements (i.e., an example from the original test data can appear more than once in a sample)." Crucially for small subgroups: the bootstrap "test is less effective for small test sets, as it assumes that the test set distribution does not deviate too much from the population distribution." — [Dror et al. 2018, §3.2](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **The exact permutation count and a practical threshold.** "the exact permutation test, for example, requires the generation of all `2^n` data permutations (where `n` is the number of points in the dataset). To overcome this, approximate randomization can be used, as was done, e.g., by Yeh (2000) for test sets of more than 20 points." — [Dror et al. 2018, §3.3](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **Which sampling-free tests, and the power ordering.** Dror et al. cover the sign test and its variants (including McNemar as the binary-classification special case) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Wilcoxon, 1945), noting Wilcoxon "is more powerful than the sign test and its variants" because it ranks the *magnitudes* of differences, and that it is used in NLP "(Søgaard et al., 2014; Søgaard, 2013; Yang and Mitchell, 2017)) due to its improved power compared to the sign test variants." — [Dror et al. 2018, §3.2](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **Documented misuse of the t-test in NLP.** The survey flags three error cases: "1. Using the t-test when the evaluation measure is not an average measure; 2. Using the t-test for a classification task (i.e. when the observations are categorical rather [than continuous]) ..." and notes the t-test "assumes the data is i.i.d." — [Dror et al. 2018, §4–5](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **Dependent test sets: the explicitly recommended NLP procedure.** "the test sets of the different folds are obviously not independent – their content is even likely to overlap. One solution we would like to propose here is based on replicability analysis (Dror et al., 2017). This paper proposes a statistical significance framework for multiple comparisons performed with dependent test sets, using the `K_Bonferroni` estimator for the number of datasets with significant effect. One statistically sound way to test for significance when a cross-validation protocol is employed is hence to calculate the p-value for each fold separately, and then to perform replicability analysis for dependent datasets with `K_Bonferroni`. Only if this analysis rejects the null hypothesis in all folds (or in more than a predefined threshold number of folds), the results should be declared significant. Here again, further statistical investigation may lead to additional, potentially better, solutions." — [Dror et al. 2018, §5](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)
- **The replicability framework itself.** Dror R, Baumer G, Bogomolov M, Reichart R (2017). "Replicability Analysis for Natural Language Processing: Testing Significance with Multiple Datasets." *TACL*. It addresses the fact that "multiple comparisons pose significant challenges to traditional statistical analysis methods in NLP and can lead to erroneous conclusions", proposing a replicability-analysis framework, demonstrated on multi-domain dependency parsing, multilingual POS tagging, cross-domain sentiment classification and word-similarity prediction. Reference implementation: <https://github.com/rtmdrr/replicability-analysis-NLP>. — [Dror et al. 2017, TACL Q17-1033](https://aclanthology.org/Q17-1033/)
- **Søgaard et al. (2014): NLP p-values are fragile and the conventional threshold is too lax.** "Significance results following current research standards are unreliable and very sensitive to sample size, covariates such as sentence length, as well as to the existence of multiple metrics." The authors "estimate that under the assumption of perfect metrics and unbiased data, a significance cut-off at approximately 0.0025 is needed to reduce the risk of false positive results to less than 5%", adding that "since in practice there is often considerable selection bias and poor metrics, this cut-off alone will not be sufficient." Full citation: Søgaard A, Johannsen A, Plank B, Hovy D, Martínez H (2014). "What's in a p-value in NLP?" *CoNLL 2014*, Baltimore, DOI 10.3115/v1/W14-1601. — [ACL Anthology W14-1601](https://aclanthology.org/W14-1601/)
- **Søgaard on effect size across datasets.** Søgaard A (2013). "Estimating effect size across datasets." NAACL-HLT 2013 — cited by Dror et al. (2018) as prior work on NLP effect sizes. — [Dror et al. 2018 bibliography](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)

### Inferences

- **Routing a paired, clustered legal-QA benchmark through the Dror protocol.** The statistic's distribution is *not* known (correctness is binary and items are not independent), so the protocol sends you to sampling-based tests. But Dror et al.'s own caveat — the bootstrap "is less effective for small test sets" — plus the clustering issue means the i.i.d. paired bootstrap of the NLP tradition is doubly inappropriate for a 21-chain / 8-document subgroup. The defensible reading is: use the Dror protocol's *routing logic* but substitute the cluster-level resampling of Q3 for the item-level resampling the NLP papers assume.
- **Multiple-comparison practice.** Neither Dror et al. (2018) nor Søgaard et al. (2014) prescribes Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg by name in the material I verified; what Dror et al. (2018) *does* prescribe for correlated test sets is the Bonferroni-based replicability estimator of Dror et al. (2017). Søgaard et al. (2014)'s ≈0.0025 threshold is itself effectively a multiplicity/selection-bias correction expressed as a stricter α. An author comparing several benchmark groups (T1…T6) can defensibly say: per-group p-values, then either (a) Holm–Bonferroni for strict family-wise control, or (b) Benjamini–Hochberg FDR if the groups are treated as a discovery screen — but should attribute the *choice* to the general multiple-testing literature, and cite Dror et al. (2017) for the NLP-specific dependent-datasets variant.
- Søgaard et al.'s finding that significance is "very sensitive to ... covariates such as sentence length" is structurally the same phenomenon as clustering: an unmodelled grouping variable that correlates with difficulty. That is a usable rhetorical bridge between the NLP and the cluster-statistics literatures.

### Gaps

- **No ACL-venue successor to Dror et al. (2018) addressing clustered items was found.** Dror and Reichart have a 2020 book-length treatment (*Statistical Significance Testing for Natural Language Processing*, Morgan & Claypool / Springer "Synthesis Lectures") which I did not verify in this session and which may contain more; I am not citing content from it because I did not read it. Treat "2018–2026 NLP guidance on clustered benchmark items" as an unfilled gap.
- I did not verify a specific NLP paper recommending Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg by name. The report should not attribute that recommendation to Dror et al. or Søgaard et al. on the strength of these notes.
- Søgaard et al.'s derivation of the 0.0025 figure (what assumptions, what prior on true effects) is from the abstract only; I did not read the paper body.

## Q6. Minimum detectable effect for paired binary outcomes

### Takeaway

For matched pairs, all the power comes from the **discordant** pairs. Connor's
(1987) normal approximation is the standard closed form and is what SAS PROC
POWER implements; inverting it gives a clean MDE rule of thumb
`δ_MDE ≈ (z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β}) · √(p_d / N)`, where `p_d` is the expected
proportion of discordant pairs. At `N = 150–225` pairs and a plausible
`p_d = 0.10–0.30`, the 80%-power MDE is roughly **6–12 percentage points** —
before any clustering penalty, which multiplies the MDE by `√DE`.

### Cited Findings

- **Symbols (as defined in SAS PROC POWER's `PAIREDFREQ` documentation).** `n₀₁` = discordant pairs where control = failure and case = success; `n₁₀` = discordant pairs where control = success and case = failure; `n_D = n₀₁ + n₁₀` = total discordant pairs; `N` = total number of pairs; `p₀₁`, `p₁₀` = proportions of each discordant type; `z_{1−α}` = standard normal quantile at level `α`; `z_power` = quantile corresponding to desired power; `Φ(·)` = standard normal CDF; `DPR = π₀₁/π₁₀` = discordant proportion ratio. — [SAS/STAT PROC POWER documentation, "Analyses in the PAIREDFREQ Statement"](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/65328/HTML/default/statug_power_details55.htm)
- **Connor (1987) normal approximation — one-sided sample size.**
  `N = { z_{1−α}·(p₁₀ + p₀₁)^(1/2) + z_power·[p₁₀ + p₀₁ − (p₀₁ − p₁₀)²]^(1/2) }² / (p₀₁ − p₁₀)²` — [SAS PROC POWER docs](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/65328/HTML/default/statug_power_details55.htm)
- **Connor (1987) — one-sided power (upper).**
  `Power = Φ( { (p₀₁ − p₁₀)·N^(1/2) − z_{1−α}·(p₁₀ + p₀₁)^(1/2) } / [p₁₀ + p₀₁ − (p₀₁ − p₁₀)²]^(1/2) )`.
  Two-sided power is the "sum of upper one-sided powers, each calculated at `α/2` instead of `α`." — [SAS PROC POWER docs](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/65328/HTML/default/statug_power_details55.htm)
- **Miettinen (1968) exact-conditional-style approximation — one-sided sample size.**
  `N = { z_{1−α}(p₁₀ + p₀₁) + z_power·[(p₁₀ + p₀₁)² − ¼(p₀₁ − p₁₀)²(3 + p₁₀ + p₀₁)]^(1/2) }² / { (p₁₀ + p₀₁)(p₀₁ − p₁₀)² }`
  and the matching power:
  `Power = Φ( { (p₀₁ − p₁₀)·[N(p₁₀ + p₀₁)]^(1/2) − z_{1−α}(p₁₀ + p₀₁) } / [(p₁₀ + p₀₁)² − ¼(p₀₁ − p₁₀)²(3 + p₁₀ + p₀₁)]^(1/2) )` — [SAS PROC POWER docs](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/65328/HTML/default/statug_power_details55.htm)
- **Which to use.** "Miettinen tends to be slightly anticonservative; Connor is slightly conservative." — [SAS PROC POWER docs](https://support.sas.com/documentation/cdl/en/statug/65328/HTML/default/statug_power_details55.htm)
- **Power lives entirely in the discordant pairs; reparameterisation via the odds ratio.** "The power of McNemar's test comes entirely from the discordant pairs"; given total discordant probability `p_d` and the odds ratio `OR` among discordant pairs, `p₁₀ = (p_d · OR)/(1 + OR)` and `p₀₁ = p_d/(1 + OR)`. An equivalent compact form of Connor's formula is `n = (z_α √p_d + z_β √(p_d − δ²))² / δ²` with `δ = p₁₀ − p₀₁` the marginal difference. — [MetricGate, "Sample Size for McNemar's Paired Test"](https://metricgate.com/docs/sample-size-mcnemar-paired) — **caveat: commercial site, not peer-reviewed; but the compact form is algebraically identical to the SAS-documented Connor formula above, which is the authoritative version.**

### Inferences (worked formulas and example — my arithmetic, from the cited Connor formula)

- **MDE rule of thumb.** Drop the `−δ²` term inside the second square root in Connor's formula (it is small when `δ² ≪ p_d`) and solve for `δ = |p₀₁ − p₁₀|` at two-sided level `α` and power `1 − β`:

  `δ_MDE ≈ (z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β}) · √(p_d / N)`,  with `p_d = p₀₁ + p₁₀`.

  At `α = 0.05` two-sided and 80% power, `z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β} = 1.960 + 0.8416 = 2.8016`.

- **Worked table (independent pairs, no clustering penalty):**

  | `N` pairs | `p_d = 0.10` | `p_d = 0.20` | `p_d = 0.30` |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 150 | 7.2 pp | 10.2 pp | 12.5 pp |
  | 225 | 5.9 pp | 8.4 pp | 10.2 pp |

  Accuracy check against the exact Connor formula: for `N = 225`, `p_d = 0.20`, `δ = 0.0835` ⇒ `p₀₁ = 0.14175`, `p₁₀ = 0.05825`, and Connor's two-sided expression returns `N = 222.8` against the 225 actually available — the approximation overstates the required `N` (equivalently, overstates `δ_MDE`) by under 1.5%, so it is mildly conservative. Use it for reporting; use the exact formula or PROC POWER / `statsmodels` for a final number.

- **Reporting sentence an author can write.** *"With `N = 225` matched pairs and an observed discordance rate of `p_d = 0.20`, the design has 80% power at `α = 0.05` (two-sided) to detect a marginal difference of about 8.4 percentage points (Connor 1987 normal approximation; `δ_MDE ≈ (z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β})√(p_d/N)`). Differences smaller than that are not resolvable at this sample size."* Note that `p_d` must be *estimated from the data or a pilot* — it is not a free choice, and a benchmark with highly similar systems will have small `p_d` (good for MDE) while a benchmark with noisy items will have large `p_d` (bad).

- **Clustering penalty on the MDE.** Because the variance of the discordant-pair difference is inflated by the design effect, and `δ_MDE ∝ 1/√N_effective`:

  `δ_MDE,clustered ≈ δ_MDE,iid · √DE`,  with `DE = 1 + (n̄_c − 1)·ρ̂`

  and `n̄_c` the adjusted mean number of **discordant pairs** per cluster — which is precisely the inflation factor `c̄ = 1 + (n̄_c − 1)ρ̂` quoted from the Obuchowski/Nam form in Q4, so the two pieces of the literature join here. Illustration (not a cited result): 225 pairs in 8 documents, `n̄_c ≈ 5.6` discordant pairs/document, `ρ̂ = 0.10` ⇒ `DE ≈ 1.46`, `√DE ≈ 1.21`, so an 8.4 pp MDE becomes ~10.2 pp. With `ρ̂ = 0.30` ⇒ `DE ≈ 2.38`, `√DE ≈ 1.54`, MDE ~12.9 pp. The penalty is driven by discordant pairs *per cluster*, not items per cluster, which is a useful and non-obvious point.

### Gaps

- I did not read Connor (1987, *Biometrics* 43:207–211, "Sample size for testing differences in proportions for the paired-sample design") or Miettinen (1968, *Biometrics* 24:339–352) directly; the formulas above are from SAS's documentation of its own implementation, which is an authoritative secondary source for the formulas but not the papers themselves. Full bibliographic details should be confirmed before citing.
- **No source found gives an MDE formula for clustered *matched pairs* jointly.** The `√DE` inflation above is my composition of two cited results (the design effect from Q1 and the Obuchowski-style discordant-pair inflation factor from Q4). It should be labelled as an approximation, not attributed to anyone. ## Q7. How to report a subgroup whose effective sample size is tiny

### Takeaway

There is no accepted NLP convention. There *is* well-established survey and
medical-reporting practice: report raw counts rather than percentages when the
denominator is small (common cutpoint: `n < 20`), and suppress or flag estimates
below an agency-set minimum denominator (examples found: `n < 20` numerator or
`n < 100` denominator flagged as "small numbers"; a minimum of 60 unweighted
observations for publication). All sources I found concede their cutpoints are
conventional rather than derived.

### Cited Findings

- **Report counts, not percentages, for small samples — and the cutpoint is admittedly arbitrary.** "When sample size is less than, say, 20, consider reporting the actual numbers rather than percentages. The cutpoint of 20 to indicate a small sample is reasonable but arbitrary." Rationale: "Especially in small samples, percentages can be misleading because the size of the percentage can be so much greater than the number it represents ... a statement that 20% of patients were treated successfully is true for 1 of 5 patients, as well as for 1000 of 5000 patients." — [*How to Report Statistics in Medicine* (ACP Press), sample pages](https://store.acponline.org/ebiz/images/ProductImages/HRSM2_samplepages_CG.pdf)
- **"Small numbers" thresholds and the reliability rationale.** "Less than 20 individuals in the numerator and/or less than 100 in the denominator (i.e., unweighted sample size) are referred to as small numbers. Estimates with small denominators or sample sizes, weighted or unweighted, are associated with greater risk of disclosure and less reliability." — [NCI Healthcare Delivery Research Program, "Small Denominator Guidelines", 2022](https://healthcaredelivery.cancer.gov/poc/POC_Small-Denominator-Guidlines_2022-08-22.pdf)
- **A published-minimum rule from a federal survey programme.** "Published estimates should be based on an unweighted sample of at least 60 persons for the subgroup represented in the total count or in the denominator of a percentage." — [AHRQ MEPS Precision Guidelines](https://meps.ahrq.gov/mepsweb/survey_comp/precision_guidelines.shtml)
- **Suppression as the standard remedy.** "When the numbers of people or families used to compute demographic statistics are small, those percentages tend to have poor reliability, and to discourage misinterpretation and misuse of unstable statistics, these statistics are not shown in tables, graphs and maps." — [NIMHD HD Pulse, suppression policy](https://hdpulse.nimhd.nih.gov/data/suppressed.html)
- **"You can report percentages with small samples, but should you?"** A practitioner-facing treatment of the same question exists in the UX-research literature. — [MeasuringU, "Should you avoid reporting percentages from small sample studies?"](https://measuringu.com/should-you-avoid-reporting-percentages-from-small-sample-studies/) — low-authority source; cited only to show the question is live outside clinical statistics.
- **Diagnostic reporting for few-cluster analyses (what the econometrics side asks you to show).** For any few-cluster bootstrap, report the bootstrap distribution's summary statistics, the replication count, the extreme values, and a histogram — and investigate any missing bootstrap t-statistics before reporting (full quote in Q3). — [Cameron & Miller 2015, §VI.C.3](http://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/Cameron_Miller_JHR_2015_February.pdf)

### Inferences

- **A defensible reporting package for a tiny-`n_eff` subgroup**, assembled from the above; each element has a cited basis, the *combination* is mine:
  1. Report **raw counts** (`x/n`) rather than percentages, per the `n < 20` convention — and apply the convention to `n_eff`, not to `n`, since `n_eff` is what carries the information.
  2. Report `k` (number of source documents), the cluster-size vector, `ρ̂`, `DE`, and `n_eff` explicitly, so the reader can see the gap between `n` and `n_eff`.
  3. Report **per-cluster results** (one line per document). This is the only presentation that survives the Donald & Lang (2007) logic, under which the document is the unit of analysis.
  4. **Widen the interval** by the design effect (equivalently compute the interval at `n_eff`), or state the exact resolution limit of the chosen test (e.g. with `k = 4`, a wild cluster bootstrap cannot produce `p < 0.125`; see Q3).
  5. **Label the subgroup non-inferential** — descriptive/diagnostic only — and say so in the table caption, not only in a footnote. Suppression is the established remedy in survey practice; for a benchmark, labelling rather than suppressing preserves the diagnostic value while blocking the misreading.
  6. State the MDE (Q6) so that "no significant difference" is not misread as "no difference".
- **Where the literature does not help.** None of the survey/medical thresholds (20, 60, 100) was derived for an effective sample size under clustering, and none is a formal rule. An author should present whichever threshold they adopt as a declared convention with a citation to precedent, not as a methodological requirement.

### Gaps

- **No NLP/IR convention for reporting tiny-`n_eff` benchmark subgroups was found.** I searched for one; nothing in the ACL-venue material I read addresses it. This should be reported as an open gap, and any convention the author adopts should be presented as their own declared policy with analogical support from survey practice.
- I did not find a source that explicitly endorses "widen the CI by `√DE` and label as non-inferential" as a package. Items 4–6 above are a reasoned composition, not a citable convention.
- The ACP *How to Report Statistics in Medicine* quote is from publisher sample pages rather than the book proper; the full citation (Lang TA, Secic M, *How to Report Statistics in Medicine*, 2nd ed., ACP Press, 2006) should be confirmed before use, as should the page.
